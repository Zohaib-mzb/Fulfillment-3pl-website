Electric delivery vans are becoming a familiar sight on city streets. For brands, they raise practical questions: are they reliable, do they cost more, what happens in January, and is it honest to tell customers your orders arrive by EV? This guide explains how EV delivery fleets work in plain terms.

## Why do EVs suit final mile delivery?

Final mile routes in a city have a particular pattern: lots of stops, short distances between them, slow traffic and a return to the same depot every day. That pattern happens to suit electric drivetrains well.

- **Stop and go driving.** Electric motors deliver power instantly and regenerative braking recovers some energy each time the van slows down.
- **No idling.** At each stop the motor isn't running, so there is no fuel burned while the driver walks to the door.
- **Predictable daily distance.** Routes are planned the night before, so range can be checked in advance.
- **Overnight charging.** Vans return to base, which makes it practical to charge them between shifts.
- **Quieter streets.** Electric vans are noticeably quieter in residential neighbourhoods, early and late in the day.

That is why EV adoption has tended to start in dense urban delivery rather than long haul freight.

## What does an EV fleet cost?

The cost picture has two halves.

**Up front**, electric vans generally cost more to buy than comparable fuel vans, and charging equipment adds to the bill. Government incentive programs have helped offset this in Canada at different times; they change, so check what federal and Ontario programs currently apply before budgeting.

**Day to day**, running costs tend to be lower. Electricity usually costs less per kilometre than fuel, and an electric drivetrain has fewer moving parts, which typically means less routine maintenance (no oil changes, for example, and less brake wear thanks to regenerative braking).

Whether an EV is cheaper overall depends on how much it is driven, the price paid, electricity rates and how long it stays in service. For a city delivery van that drives a full route most days, the running cost advantage has more time to add up.

## How is range managed?

Range is the question everyone asks, and planning is the answer.

### Route level planning

Every route has a known length and stop count before the van leaves. A good planner checks each route against the van assigned to it, with a margin for traffic, detours and weather. Routes that would stretch a van too far are split or reassigned.

### Winter

Cold weather reduces usable range: batteries are less efficient in the cold, and heating the cab uses energy. Fleets handle this by increasing their safety margin in winter, prewarming vans while they are still plugged in, and planning slightly shorter routes on the coldest days.

### Charging

Most charging happens overnight at the depot, when vans are idle anyway. Some fleets add a midday top up between route waves. The goal is that charging fits around the delivery schedule, not the other way round.

## What does "EV first" mean?

You will see fleets describe themselves in different ways, so it is worth being precise:

- **All electric** means every vehicle in the fleet is electric.
- **EV first** means electric vehicles are assigned routes first, and other vehicles cover routes that exceed range or extra volume on busy days.

## What does EV delivery mean for my brand?

### A specific, honest story

"Your local orders are delivered in electric vans wherever the route allows" is a concrete statement customers can understand. It is far more credible than vague claims about being "green".

### Be careful with environmental claims

Canadian competition law expects environmental claims to be substantiated, and customers are increasingly sceptical of broad promises. Avoid phrases like "carbon neutral delivery" or "zero emissions shipping" unless you can back them up with a documented method. Stick to what is true and checkable: which deliveries are made by EV, and in which areas.

### It fits with fast local delivery

EV delivery works best on dense local routes, which are also where next day and same day delivery make the most sense. That means you don't have to choose between speed and a lower emission option for your local customers. ## Common myths about EV delivery

**"Electric vans can't handle a full day of deliveries."** City routes are planned in advance, and a well planned route sits comfortably within a van's range, with a margin for weather. Problems come from unplanned routes, not from the vehicles themselves.

**"EVs are only for small parcels."** Electric vans come in a range of sizes. What limits a route is usually stop count and parcel volume, the same as for any van.

**"Switching to EVs means slower delivery."** Delivery speed depends on where stock is held, cutoffs and route planning. An EV first fleet can run next day and same day routes just like any other. The vehicle is not the bottleneck.

**"It's all marketing."** It can be, if claims are vague. Specific, checkable statements about which deliveries are electric are what separate a real operational choice from a slogan.

## Questions to ask a delivery partner about EVs

- What share of my deliveries would be made by electric vehicles, and in which areas?
- How do you plan for range in winter?
- What happens on a route that exceeds a van's range?
- Can you provide information I can use to describe my deliveries accurately?
- Is emissions reporting available, and how is it calculated?
