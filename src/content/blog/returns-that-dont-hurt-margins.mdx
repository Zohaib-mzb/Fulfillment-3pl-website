Returns are part of selling online. In some categories, apparel and footwear especially, they are part of the business model. The good news is that most of the cost of a return isn't fixed. It comes from delay, inconsistency and missing information, and all three can be fixed with a clear process. Here is a simple playbook you can put in place whether you handle returns yourself or with a partner.

## Where does the money go in a return?

Before fixing anything, it helps to see where value leaks out:

- **Transport.** Getting the item back from the customer.
- **Handling.** Opening, inspecting, repackaging and putting away.
- **Lost value.** An item that waits weeks to be processed may miss its season, or be discounted to clear.
- **Customer service time.** Every "where is my refund?" email costs someone's attention.
- **Lost customers.** A painful return experience can lose the next order too.

The playbook below targets each of these.

## Step 1: Write a policy people can actually understand

Your returns policy is a customer experience document and a cost control tool. Make it short and specific:

- How many days customers have to start a return.
- What condition items must be in.
- Which items can't be returned (for hygiene or safety reasons, for example).
- Whether you offer refunds, exchanges, store credit or all three.
- Who pays for return shipping, and when.

Clear rules reduce disputes and set expectations before anything is shipped back.

## Step 2: Make starting a return easy

A returns portal or a simple form linked from the order confirmation saves everyone time. Ask customers for the order number, the items, and a reason from a short list. That reason code is gold later on, so keep the list consistent and specific: "too small", "too large", "arrived damaged", "not as described", "changed my mind".

## Step 3: Get the parcel back quickly

Speed matters from the moment the customer decides to return something. The longer an item sits in a hallway, the more likely it is to be damaged or forgotten.

Doorstep pickup is the most convenient option for customers who live in your delivery area: no printer, no trip to a post office. For customers further away, a prepaid label is the usual approach. If many of your customers are in the GTA, local pickup can shorten the return trip considerably, our [returns service](/services/returns) works this way.

## Step 4: Grade every return the same way

Inconsistent inspection is a hidden cost. If one person restocks an item that another would have discarded, your inventory quality drifts and customers notice.

Agree on simple grades:

- **Sellable**: back into stock as new.
- **Repack**: sellable after new packaging, a tidy fold or a fresh tag.
- **Not sellable**: set aside to donate, recycle, return to the supplier or dispose of responsibly.

Write the rules down with photos of examples. Then the grade is a decision, not an opinion.

## Step 5: Restock fast

Once an item is graded sellable, get it back into its bin location and update your stock count. That is the moment a return stops being a cost and becomes inventory again.

The connection between returns and inventory is why it helps to process returns in the same place your stock is stored. When [fulfillment](/services/fulfillment) and returns happen under one roof, a restocked item can be sold to the next customer the same day.

## Step 6: Use exchanges and store credit well

A refund ends the relationship for now; an exchange keeps it going. For fit related returns, make exchanges the easiest path: suggest the next size, reserve it, and ship it as soon as the original is on its way back, or once it is received and graded, if you prefer more control.

Store credit with a small bonus can also work well, as long as it is genuinely useful to the customer.

## Step 7: Read the reason codes every month

Reason codes turn returns from a cost centre into product feedback:

- A spike in "too small" on one style suggests the size chart needs work.
- "Arrived damaged" on fragile items points to packaging.
- "Not as described" usually means photos or copy need updating.

Fixing the root cause is the cheapest return of all, the one that never happens. Brands in fit sensitive categories like [apparel and footwear](/industries/apparel-footwear) tend to see the biggest gains here.

## How should returns work in peak season?

The weeks after a big sale or the holidays bring a wave of returns at exactly the moment teams are tired. Plan for it:

- **Extend return windows on purpose, not by accident.** Holiday gift returns often arrive weeks later; say so clearly in your policy.
- **Book extra processing capacity** in January, so returns don't pile up for weeks.
- **Prioritize high value and seasonal items** for inspection first: they lose value fastest.
- **Communicate refund timing** so customers aren't left wondering.

A returns backlog is one of the quietest ways margins erode. Handling peaks deliberately keeps sellable stock moving.

## What should I measure?

You don't need a complex dashboard. Track a handful of numbers over time:

- Return rate by product and by reason.
- Days from return request to item received.
- Days from item received to restocked or refunded.
- Share of returns that end in an exchange or store credit.
- Share of returned items graded sellable.

When those numbers move in the right direction, margins follow.

## The short version

Make returns easy to start, fast to collect, consistent to grade and quick to restock, and listen to what the reasons are telling you.
