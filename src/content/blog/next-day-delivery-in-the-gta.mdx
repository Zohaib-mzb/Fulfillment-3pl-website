"Arrives tomorrow" is one of the most persuasive lines you can put on a product page. For brands selling to customers in Toronto and the surrounding cities, it is also very achievable, if the pieces behind it are set up properly. This guide explains what next day delivery actually requires, where it tends to break, and how to offer it honestly so it helps rather than hurts.

## What does next day delivery actually mean?

Next day delivery is a promise with a condition attached: **orders received before a cutoff are delivered the following day.** Everything depends on that cutoff. An order at 9 a.m. and an order at 11 p.m. are very different problems.

So when a provider says "next day", the useful questions are:

- What is the cutoff, and is it based on when the order is placed or when the parcel reaches the sort centre?
- Which postal codes are covered?
- Which days of the week does it run?

Get those answers in writing, and design your checkout messaging around them.

## What has to happen behind the scenes?

Next day is a chain. Each link needs to hold.

### 1. Stock has to be close to the customer

If inventory sits in another province, a GTA customer's parcel has a long haul trip before the final mile even starts. Holding stock in a Toronto area [fulfillment centre](/services/fulfillment) takes that leg out completely for local orders.

### 2. Orders have to be packed the same day

Orders that arrive before the packing cutoff need to be picked, packed and labelled that day. That requires enough capacity for normal days *and* busy ones, and a process that doesn't stall when one order is complicated.

### 3. Parcels have to be sorted overnight

Packed parcels are scanned, grouped by delivery area and assigned to routes. The fewer handoffs between networks, the fewer chances for delay.

### 4. Routes have to be planned, not improvised

Good routes group stops by neighbourhood, respect building access rules and fit the vehicle that will drive them. That is what makes delivery times predictable.

### 5. The doorstep has to go right

Live tracking, clear delivery notes and a photo at the door make the final moment smooth, and give you evidence if a customer later asks where their parcel went. You can see how we handle each step on the [final mile delivery](/services/final-mile-delivery) page.

## Why does a fast delivery promise help conversion?

Shoppers weigh more than price. Near the end of checkout, delivery is often the last open question: *when will this actually arrive?* An unclear answer such as "3 to 7 business days" invites hesitation, especially for gifts or items someone needs soon.

A specific promise works differently. "Order in the next two hours, get it tomorrow" is concrete. It removes uncertainty, gives a reason to buy now rather than later, and signals that the business behind the store is organized. This is a qualitative effect rather than a fixed percentage, and it varies by product and audience, which is why it is worth testing for your own store rather than relying on someone else's numbers.

There is also a downstream benefit. When delivery is fast and visible, customers send fewer "where is my order?" messages, and your support team spends less time chasing parcels.

## How do I show delivery promises honestly?

A delivery promise only helps if you keep it. A few principles:

- **Show promises by location.** Use postal code or zone based rates so next day appears only where it is genuinely available.
- **Be precise about cutoffs.** "Order by 11 a.m." is better than "fast shipping".
- **Don't promise weekends you can't deliver.** If your network doesn't run Sundays, a Saturday afternoon order isn't next day.
- **Have a plan for peak season.** If capacity tightens in late November and December, adjust messaging early.
- **Send tracking automatically.** The promise continues after checkout.

## Where does next day delivery break?

Most failures come from a handful of places:

- **Late cutoffs that the warehouse can't meet** on busy days.
- **Handoffs between carriers**, where a parcel waits for the next pickup or scan.
- **Failed first attempts**: locked buildings, missing buzzer codes, nobody home for a parcel that can't be left.
- **Address problems**, especially in new build neighbourhoods.

Ask any provider how they handle each one. A clear plan for failed deliveries (notify the customer, reattempt or hold for pickup) matters as much as the speed of the first attempt.

## How do I know if it's working?

Measure a few things before and after you introduce next day:

- Checkout completion rate for customers who see a next day option.
- The share of orders delivered by the promised date.
- "Where is my order?" contacts per hundred orders.
- Reviews or comments that mention delivery.

If you can, test messaging with a portion of traffic first. The goal is a promise you keep almost every time.

## Next day or same day?

Next day is the right default for most GTA orders: it is fast, predictable and efficient to run. [Same day delivery](/services/same-day-delivery) is the upgrade for moments when timing matters most: gifts, replacements and items someone has just run out of. Many brands offer both, with next day as standard and same day for orders placed by late morning.
